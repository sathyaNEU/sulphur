Verbatim feedback:

> What's good:
> This is a serious Project Sulfur record linkage task. The scenario is real health information management work: two
> patient indexes (calder_bay_patient_index_2026-11-02.csv and rosario_patient_index_20261102.csv) converting into one
> EMPI, four candidate rules from one_chart_empi_conversion_plan.pdf section 7.2, and a policy line in
> patient_identity_policy_him-14.pdf section 3 of no more than one expected false merge per 2,000 automatic links. The
> data package is rich and well connected: the blood bank type and screen file gives an independent check on false
> links, the labor and delivery log documents twins, the Calder Bay merge history exposes the AUTO merges from March
> 2019 that joined on last name, birth date and sex alone, and the name equivalence table lets a solver tell nickname
> forms from different people. The national match rates in halvard_match_6_specification.pdf are contradicted by the
> local data, which is exactly the kind of local versus reference rate judgment this domain is built on.
>
> The task holds up on determinism and correctness. The rule link counts reproduce exactly from the shipped data files
> (Rule A 71,728, Rule B 39,810, Rule D 46,046), the policy wording ("expected false merges") pins the call, and the
> committed answer, run Rule B at about 0.25 per 2,000, is the call the data supports. The supplementary asks (distinct
> patients, patients both indexes hold, Calder Bay records holding two or more patients, residual duplicates after Rule
> B) each need genuine modelling and are banded sensibly.
>
> What's bad:
> The task is not difficult enough. Strong test responses reached most of the requested scorecard. The reasons are in
> the task itself:
> - Three of the four rules fail by a wide margin on directly countable evidence. Rule C joins 45 documented twin pairs
>   from the delivery log against an allowance of about 28 (56,207 / 2,000); Rule D joins 76 against about 23 (46,046 /
>   2,000). Rule A has 16 blood-discordant pairs with different SSNs among its links that Rule B does not make, against
>   about 0.3 expected under the national rates, so it fails under any estimate consistent with the data (allowance
>   about 35.9). A solver does not need a careful false-merge model to rule these out.
> - Once A, C and D are out, the main decision collapses to a binary: Rule B or no rule. The test responses reproduced
>   the link counts, matched the Rule C figures, put A and D over the line, and named Rule B as "the strongest
>   candidate", with their own central estimate at 0.411 per 2,000. They only stopped short of approving it because of
>   a reserve for "seven unresolved blood-discordant pairs". So almost all of the analytical work lands, and the
>   remaining difficulty rests on one step: resolving Rule B's eight blood-group mismatches (six of them share an SSN and
>   sit on Calder Bay AUTO-merge survivors, so they are overlays, not false links).
> - The link counts themselves are mechanical once the standardization rules in the specification are applied, so a
>   large share of the deliverable is reachable without any judgment.
>
> What needs fixing:
> - Widen the analytical work beyond the B or no-rule call, so the margins on A, C and D are not obvious from a direct
>   count.
> - Reconcile the committed step 4 figure for Rule A's typed pairs: the committed answer says 30 of 8,043 differ in
>   blood group, while the shipped blood bank file gives 32 of 8,043.
> - In the committed memo and step 4, state plainly how Rule B's eight blood-mismatched pairs are resolved (which are
>   same-SSN overlay pairs on Calder Bay AUTO-merge survivors). The current line that a raw blood reading "would condemn
>   Rule B too (97)" invites the opposite call without showing why it is wrong.
>
> How to fix it:
> Keep the data, the policy line and the determinism. Then move more of the decision onto estimation where a reasonable
> but shallow approach lands on the wrong side:
> 1. Tune the data so Rule A's local false-merge estimate sits close to its allowance of about 35.9, so the call on A
>    depends on modelling chance agreement by birth cohort and surname community correctly, not on a 16 versus 0.3
>    contrast.
> 2. Bring Rules C and D nearer the line on documented twins alone, so the estimate has to include the twins the
>    delivery log does not show (separate-visit registrations, nickname pairs at one address) to decide them.
> 3. Ask for Rule B's resolved false merges as a stated figure, with the count of blood-discordant B pairs explained as
>    overlays or shared SSNs and the count left as true false links, so the deciding step is a checkable result rather
>    than a reserve judgment.
>
> Ways to make the task harder:
> 1. Ask for a second checkable result on Rule B: how many of its 39,810 links involve a Calder Bay record that holds
>    two patients, and how many of those would need a split before linking.
> 2. Narrow Rule A's margin so that the national rates (about 5 false merges) and the local cohort model fall on
>    opposite sides of the allowance by a modest amount, and the blood-group check only tests the estimate rather than
>    settling it.
> 3. Make the blood-typed subset unrepresentative in a way that matters (typed patients already skew about 8 years
>    older than Rule A's linked patients) and ask for a reweighted blood-based estimate by birth decade.
> 4. Ask for the expected false merges of a fifth, combined rule (for example Rule B plus Rule D links restricted to
>    records whose first names match under halvard_name_equivalence_table.csv).
> 5. Use halvard_pilot_review_2026.xlsx as genuine evidence by asking for a precision estimate for the review band.
>
> These are general suggestions for strengthening the task, not a guaranteed stump. Please test your own model rollouts
> and only resubmit if at least two strong models still miss the answer.
