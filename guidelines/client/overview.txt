Build a rigorous analytical task with one defensible answer.
You write analytical tasks that stump frontier models: a precise decision over messy, multi-source real files, with one deterministic answer.

Welcome
Welcome to Project Sulfur! Your task is to write a realistic analytical task: a request from a stakeholder who has to make one decision, built on real, messy files that point to one defensible answer. You write the prompt, gather the input files, and prepare the golden output that answers it.

Your goal
Write a task that is hard enough that the average of the two model runs scores below 60% against the rubric, and the runs lose for a genuine analytical reason. The difficulty has to come from deep reasoning: a chain of dependent inferences that includes a real statistical or modeling judgment. It can never come from a planted defect, a field read one particular way, or a procedure the files already spell out. Every task ends in one deterministic recommendation that careful analysts would all reach from the files.

Approval criteria
Approval criteria
How your task gets approved
Every submission is reviewed within 24-48 hours. To be approved, it has to clear all of the following.
The two runs average below 60% against the rubric
Two model runs are generated from your prompt, and a rubric is generated automatically from your prompt and your answer. The average of the two run scores must be below 60%; exactly 60% does not pass, and one run may score higher as long as the average is under 60%. You edit the rubric only when a criterion is clearly wrong or missing.
The solution is deterministic
One unambiguous conclusion that domain experts would independently agree on, with every term that could move it pinned by the prompt or forced by the data.
The task fits its assigned domain
The deciding step needs your assigned domain's own reasoning, not just its vocabulary. If swapping the nouns for another field leaves the task unchanged, the domain is only a backdrop.
The runs fail for a deep-reasoning reason
The deciding step takes three or more dependent inferences, at least one a real statistical or modeling judgment, and the runs lose on that judgment (see Deep reasoning). The task cannot be won or lost on a banned stump: rule application, data semantics, a planted defect or decoy, a replayed or flipped number, a single fact, or an unpinned ambiguity. Formatting slips, alternative wording, a broken golden, a missing file or a grader failure never count as difficulty.
The golden solution is correct
Every number and step must be reproducible from the prompt and the shipped input files.
The input files clear the bar
At least 10 real, usable files (system files such as .DS_Store do not count) in at least 2 file categories, including at least 2 large, messy files and at least 2 distractor files. Nothing LLM generated, and no file that reveals how the task was built. Cleaning the mess must never be what decides the answer.
The output deliverables clear the bar
The prompt asks for one deterministic main recommendation and one to three files in realistic formats, with a few hard, multi-part asks rather than a long list of easy ones. The golden solution is one ZIP with the same number of files the prompt requests, one consistent set of numbers, and nothing that looks LLM generated.
The prompt is original
It cannot closely resemble another fellow's task or one you have already submitted.

The input package needs real depth:

At least 10 usable files, usually 10 to 30, in at least 2 file categories. System files such as .DS_Store or __MACOSX do not count.
At least 2 messy, complicated, large files: one CSV that runs 10 to 20 pages, and one CSV with 20,000+ rows. Both must carry realistic mess that has to be cleaned and reconciled.
At least 2 distractor files. A distractor is not needed or used in your solution but looks like it might be useful. A completely unrelated file is not a distractor.
Files must be real and license-clean, with source, date, and license recorded.

Deliverables and asks
One to three deliverables. You are not assigned a required format family. Choose the files a real analyst would actually produce for the decision, and match the format to the work: a forecast may need a runnable script, a certification may call for a CSV, and a board decision may be best as a short memo. Do not default to analysis_report.pdf for every task.
A visual is optional. Use one when it makes the decision easier to communicate or understand. A chart, waterfall, matrix, heatmap, or table can give the stakeholder the main read first. A table does not need to be a separate file; it can appear inside a memo, workbook, or script output.
No cap on asks, and each one is hard. There is no target number you need to hit. Each ask should do real analytical work and be multi-dimensional: one ask can cover many rows, periods, segments, or calculations. The task still resolves to one deterministic recommendation.

Give the answer real structure
A concise prompt can still ask for a rich answer. The work should come from the structure of the answer and a few demanding asks, not from a long list of simple requirements: a ranked list under a budget, a bridge between two totals, a grid of cells, a forecast across many periods. The rubric is generated from that structure, so a well-shaped answer gives the runs many places to show whether they did the analysis.

The shapes below are examples, not a fixed menu. Use a different structure if it fits your data and decision better. Whatever shape you choose, the deciding step must still be an analytical judgment, not a procedure the documents spell out.

Open any shape to see what it means, then read prompts for every domain on Example prompts.


01
Ranked list under a cap
You rank every candidate on one score and fill the list from the top until a cap runs out. The result is the set that makes the cut, the first candidate left below the line, and the margin. The shape earns its place when building the score takes judgment, for example shrinking noisy small-sample rates or adjusting for case mix, so a simpler score reorders the list near the line.

02
Forecast across many periods
You forecast a value for each of many periods, then commit to one level for the whole series. The result is one committed level. The hard part should be the model itself: justifying it out of sample against a naive baseline, handling immature recent periods, and carrying the forecast uncertainty into the committed level.

03
Bridge between two totals
You walk one total across to another, one reconciling item at a time, until the two sides meet: two systems, two periods, or plan against actual. The answer is the figure you adopt and the item that moves the total most. Keep the bridge analytical, such as separating a mix shift from a rate change, rather than a hunt for one planted discrepancy.

Setting one dial

Sequenced schedule under capacity

07
Grid of cells
You fill a grid of rows against columns, like segments against options, with one measured value in each cell. The answer is the best pick for each row or the one column you adopt everywhere. The grid works when the cells disagree for a reason the analyst has to explain, such as subgroups responding in opposite directions that a pooled figure hides.

Rule replayed on history
You replay a decided rule across every period of a labeled history to see how it would have done, then size what it frees up for the coming year. A replay on its own is banned as the deciding stump. It works only when an independent second analytical step, such as projecting the coming year out of sample or separating real change from drift in the history, is what flips the answer.

The difficulty bar: The two runs average below 60%
A task passes when the average of the two runs is below 60% against the rubric. Exactly 60% is too easy. One run can score higher as long as the average stays under. You record both scores and the average in the Reward step.

Low scores are necessary, not sufficient. Reviewers read where each run first went wrong. A run that took a reasonable method that does not go far enough, stopped a chain of inference short, or set aside its own evidence is a fair stump. A run that missed one planted detail, misread a clause or misapplied a written rule is not, and the task goes back even under 60%. See Deep reasoning.


Show how the rubric is built
The rubric is generated from your prompt, your final recommendation, your supplementary answers and your critical components. Do not edit it unless a criterion is clearly wrong or missing.

Part of the rubric	What it covers
Recommendation and critical components	The deterministic call and the load-bearing figures it rests on. These carry the most weight.
Supplementary questions and asks	Each hard ask you wrote. A wrong analytical path should get them wrong.
Instruction-following	The requested files and their output shape: file present and named, the rows and columns asked for, page length. A small share of the weight.
Not allowed: the "flip the wrong number" trap
The difficulty has to come from honest data. The reported numbers in the task should be correct, and the challenge should come from the analysis: the comparison, the model, the uncertainty, the projection.

Hidden subgroup	Mobile sessions secretly include background wakes; removing them reverses the result. The data were made misleading on purpose. Definition swap	The ranking flips once you notice the offer is paid per seat, not per logo.	Both metrics are measured correctly, and the decision turns on whether the per-seat lift persists past the novelty period, which needs a model of how usage decays by cohort.
Coverage gap	Half the period is hidden in a third extract; finding it changes the answer.	All data are present, but recent cohorts have not matured. The ranking holds only after projecting their outcomes from older cohorts' curves.

The blocks in order
Every task has the same fifteen blocks. Two model runs are generated from your prompt, a rubric is generated from your prompt and your answer, and the task passes when the average of the two runs is below 60% against that rubric, with both runs losing for a genuine analytical reason. See Deep reasoning for what that reason looks like.

Set up

Welcome. The goal and the bar: two runs that average below 60% and fail for analytical reasons. Pay is per approved task.
Domain. Your assigned domain, one of 21. The deciding step must need that field's reasoning, not just its labels (Domains).
Stumped Understanding. Confirm the number one goal: stump the models fairly, on method or analysis, never on semantics, a planted defect or a written rule.
Write a Prompt and Upload Input Files. A natural prompt asking for one main recommendation, 1 to 3 realistic files and a few hard, multi-part asks. One ZIP with 10+ real, usable files in 2+ categories, including 2+ large messy files and 2+ distractors (Prompt, Input files).
Model runs

Model Response Generation Notice. Two responses are about to be generated and can take more than 20 minutes. Keep working on the blocks below.
RLE Multi-Model Rollout. The two runs execute on your prompt and ZIP. Nothing to configure.
Your answer

Final Recommendation. Your one deterministic answer to the main recommendation only.
Supplementary Questions. A numbered list answering every other ask, one number per sub-ask, describing how each requested file should look.
Updated Critical Components. The 2 to 5 figures or facts any valid method must compute to reach the recommendation.
Updated Step-by-Step. A short numbered path from raw files to the recommendation and supplementary answers: the method and the figure at each step (Golden output).
Grade and submit

Rubric. A rubric is generated for you. You do not edit it unless a criterion is clearly wrong or missing.
Model responses. Read both runs against your answer and confirm they lost on judgment, not on formatting or a misread.
Rubric Output. Review the generated criteria, then press "Run evaluation" to grade both responses.
Reward. Record both run scores and the average. The average must be below 60%; exactly 60% does not pass.
Golden Solution. One ZIP with your finished answer files, the same number of files the prompt requests, business-realistic and not LLM-looking.
