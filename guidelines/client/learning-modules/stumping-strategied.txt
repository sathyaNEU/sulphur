Stumping strategies
Layer 2 of every task. What a fair stump looks like, what is banned, and deep-reasoning strategies for each group of domains.

A fair stump is a reasonable analysis that does not go far enough. The models clean the data and read the prompt correctly, often notice the key issue, and then lose on judgment: they take a simpler method that moves the deciding figure, stop a chain of inference before it reaches the decision, or set aside a signal their own output already showed. The deciding step takes three or more dependent analytical inferences, and at least one of them is a statistical or modeling judgment the analyst has to justify from the data. The full standard, with real tasks that meet it, is on Deep reasoning.

This is Layer 2 of Two-layer task design. The data work is Layer 1, on Data messiness: it makes the task realistic, but it never decides the answer.

How the runs should fail
When you read the two runs, the shortfall should look like one of these. Each is evidence that the task is deep.

Method shortcut. The run uses the right data but a simpler method, and the simpler method moves the deciding figure. Example: a pooled before and after comparison where the design calls for a comparison against units not yet treated.
Unfinished chain. The early steps are right, but the run does not carry the inference through. Example: it projects immature cohorts correctly, then sizes the decision on the raw, unprojected figures.
Dismissed own evidence. The run's own output shows the signal, and it sets the signal aside. Example: it prints subgroup effects in opposite directions, then recommends on the pooled average.
Self-confirming check. The run tests robustness only by varying nearby choices, so the check can never overturn the first answer. Example: it reruns the same naive model with a different window instead of testing the assumption the answer depends on.
Not evidence of a deep task
Formatting slips, a near miss on a tight threshold, a different but defensible answer the prompt allows, or a missing file do not count. Neither does a run that misread one field or skipped one instruction.

The bar: the average of the two runs must score below 60% against the auto-generated rubric, and the runs must fall short for one of the reasons above. Exactly 60% is too easy. You do not edit the rubric unless a criterion is clearly wrong or missing.

Banned as the deciding stump
Send-backs
The task must not be won or lost on any of these. Several of them side by side are still banned.

Rule application. The deciding step is a procedure the materials already spell out: a standard, manual or policy that lays out the decisive calculation; combination or precedence rules ("if the top option breaks the cap, move to the next one that fits"); a decision rule that only needs the right metric plugged in; a clause justified only by citing a real regulation.
Data semantics. Reading one field, definition, clause, window or population a particular way.
Planted defects and decoys. One planted duplicate, test rows, a timezone error, a hidden file, a mislabeled column, a wrong denominator, or a memo written to carry the wrong answer.
Replay. Re-deriving a published figure, or a task whose only twist is that a stated number is wrong.
Single-fact surface stumps. A task that becomes routine once the solver is told one fact.
Unpinned ambiguity. Two defensible readings or methods that give different answers.
Two quick tests
Rule application test: if the solver followed the written procedure literally, would the answer follow with no further analytical judgment? Then it is rule application. One-fact test: if telling the solver one fact ("that file is a sample", "exclude the test rows", "use fiscal quarters") would make the task routine, it is a surface stump. A deep task survives both: even a solver told every fact and every rule still has to make and justify the analytical judgment.

Strategies by domain group
Open your group to see deep-reasoning strategies for each domain in it: the judgment that decides, the reasonable but insufficient analysis a capable analyst is tempted to stop at, and what in the data makes the deeper method the only defensible one.


Product Analytics
Product Analytics
Product experiments rarely fail on arithmetic. They fail when the readout answers a slightly different question from the decision: an early lift instead of a lasting one, an average over users the launch will not serve, an interval that assumes more independence than the design gave. The runs will compute a correct number. Make the task turn on whether it is the right number.

01Novelty that keeps getting topped up
The judgment
Is the measured lift a durable change in behavior or a reaction to something new? Answering that means indexing the effect by time since each user's first exposure, not by calendar week, and estimating where it settles.

Scenario
A music streaming app tests a redesigned home screen for five weeks. Users enter the test the first time they open the app in the window, so every week brings a new wave of first exposures. The launch bar is a lasting gain of at least 2% in weekly listening minutes.

Why the tempting answer fails
Pooled lift of +5.1% with a tight interval, plus the standard novelty check: a calendar-week plot of the lift, which stays flat at about 5%. The flat line looks like proof that nothing is wearing off. It is flat because each week's average mixes newly exposed users at the top of their spike with earlier users whose lift has faded.

How to build it
Make entry staggered, with a steady flow of new entrants, so the calendar view stays flat by construction. Let the lift by week since first exposure fall from about +12% to about +1.2% by week four, then hold. Include a cohort of brand-new signups, who never saw the old screen and so have nothing to react to as new; their lift (about +1.1%) gives an independent read of the lasting effect. The answer is pinned when the fitted settling level and the new-signup cohort both sit clearly below 2%.

02A flat average over segments moving in opposite directions
The judgment
Does a near-zero pooled effect hide segments that respond in opposite directions, and at what segment mix should the decision be weighed? The segments must be defined before assignment, and the analyst has to separate a real split from noise found by slicing many ways.

Scenario
A secondhand marketplace tests ranking listings by predicted relevance instead of recency. Pooled conversion moves +0.3%, not significant. Split by tenure measured before assignment, new buyers gain about 4.8% and established buyers lose about 2.1%. The growth plan for the next two quarters raises the new-buyer share of traffic from 22% to 45%.

Why the tempting answer fails
"No effect, do not ship." A more careful run slices by a dozen attributes, correctly worries about multiple comparisons, and dismisses the tenure split as one of many noisy cuts. That caution is a real virtue, which is why the answer feels responsible.

How to build it
Make the tenure effects large enough to survive partial pooling across all 12 candidate segments: shrinkage pulls the other splits toward zero and leaves tenure standing. Put the forward traffic mix in a plan document as a business fact. The effect reweighted to 45% new buyers is about +1.0%, against a launch bar of +0.5%, and it stays above the bar across the plausible range of the shrinkage estimate.

03Clustered randomization with a few giant clusters
The judgment
Match the uncertainty to the unit that was randomized when the metric is a ratio and cluster sizes are very unequal.

Scenario
A team collaboration tool randomizes 420 workspaces to an AI meeting-summary feature. The metric is the share of meetings that produce a follow-up task. Six enterprise workspaces hold 38% of all meetings, and five of them landed in treatment. The prompt's bar is to ship if the lower bound of the 95% interval on the lift is above zero.

Why the tempting answer fails
A meeting-level comparison gives +3.4 points with p far below 0.001. A careful run clusters standard errors by user and still gets p around 0.004. Clustering was applied, so the result feels defended.

How to build it
Give each workspace a pre-period follow-up rate, and make the giant workspaces high-baseline accounts, so most of the raw gap is who landed where. Analysis at the workspace level, with the delta method or a workspace bootstrap for the ratio and adjustment for pre-period rates, gives about +0.6 points (95% interval roughly -0.8 to 2.0). Randomization inference over the 420 workspaces agrees, which pins the call: do not ship.

04Looks deep but is banned: the SDK release that double-fired an event
Why this is banned
A task asks why checkout conversion fell 11% in week 34. The release notes, buried among other files, mention an SDK update that started firing the checkout-view event twice on Android. Deduplicate, and conversion is flat. It feels like detective work, but it hits two bans: a planted defect decides the answer, and it fails the one-fact test, since telling the solver "Android checkout views double-fired after the update" makes the task routine. To make it deep, keep the double firing as ordinary Layer 1 cleaning that every run will handle, and leave a real 4% decline behind it. Then make the deciding step whether that decline is mix shift or a within-segment drop. A paid campaign brought lower-intent traffic in the same weeks, while a price change in one region gives a natural comparison. The run has to decompose by acquisition cohort, show within-cohort conversion fell only in the repriced region, and size the price effect on its own.

Marketing & Consumer Research
Marketing data is full of numbers that measure association and get read as cause: who redeemed, what the platform credited, what respondents said they would buy. The deep step is nearly always building the counterfactual the number leaves out, and then carrying it into a decision about spend, targeting or launch.

05Targeting the persuadable instead of the likely
The judgment
Should targeting rank customers by how likely they are to respond, or by how much the offer changes their behavior? Only the second answers a budget question, and it needs randomized history and its own validation.

Scenario
A meal-kit company plans a win-back discount for 60,000 lapsing customers. Last year's campaign held out a random 20%. The bar is a cost per incremental reactivation under $45.

Why the tempting answer fails
Train a response model on last year's redeemers (out-of-sample AUC 0.79) and send the offer to the 60,000 highest scores. It projects 14,000 redemptions, counts each as a save, and clears the bar easily. The model validates well, so the plan looks careful.

How to build it
In the randomized history, make the top-scored customers reactivate at about 38% without any offer, so most redemptions are sure things. Give a recently complained segment negative uplift, because the email reminds them to cancel. Uplift estimated from the randomized campaign and checked with a Qini curve on a held-out fold peaks near 35,000 offers. Past that point, incremental reactivations fall. Make the gap between the response ranking and the uplift ranking large on the held-out fold, so the right depth and audience are not a close call.

06Platform-credited conversions against a market test with few markets
The judgment
Estimate a channel's incremental return from a geo test with only a handful of treated markets: build a credible counterfactual from the pre-period and judge significance by permutation, not by a large-sample formula.

Scenario
A home-fitness brand's connected-TV vendor reports 9,400 view-through conversions in a quarter. The brand also went dark on connected TV in 10 of 60 markets for eight weeks. The bar is an incremental cost per acquisition under $90.

Why the tempting answer fails
Deduplicate the claimed conversions across channels with a position-based multi-touch model. Connected TV keeps about 4,100 conversions at a $61 cost per acquisition, so keep it. Deduplication feels like the principled fix for overcounting. Another shallow path compares dark and lit markets directly, scaled by population.

How to build it
Have the vendor pick the dark markets, and make them smaller and faster-growing in the pre-period, so a direct comparison is biased. A synthetic control built on weekly pre-period sales tracks them closely. Its post-period gap implies about 1,300 incremental conversions, a cost per acquisition near $190. A placebo test across the 50 lit markets ranks the real gap as extreme. Two checks pin it: the fit is tight before the test, and the decision does not change across reasonable donor pools.

07Stated intent that has to be calibrated against your own launches
The judgment
Turn a concept test's purchase intent into a trial forecast with a calibration fitted to the company's own history and validated out of sample, then carry it through planned awareness and distribution.

Scenario
A beverage company tests a functional soda concept: 61% top-two-box intent. The launch goes ahead if year-one trial reaches 8% of households. The company has 14 prior launches with concept scores and actual panel trial.

Why the tempting answer fails
Apply a textbook weighting of "definitely" and "probably" answers from a vendor primer, multiply by planned awareness and distribution, and get 10.5%: go. It is a recognized industry heuristic, so it reads as standard practice.

How to build it
In the 14 prior launches, make intent overstate trial far more for products in new categories than for line extensions. Fit the relationship with that interaction and check it by leave-one-out; its errors should be clearly smaller than the heuristic's. Carry the forecast through the planned awareness and distribution ramp, and year-one trial comes out near 6.4% (leave-one-out range about 5.1 to 7.7), wholly below 8%: no go.

08Looks deep but is banned: the attribution policy that computes itself
Why this is banned
A task ships an "attribution policy" PDF: 40% of credit to the first touch, 40% to the last, 20% spread across the middle, and any coupon-site touch within 24 hours of purchase hands its credit to the prior touch. It then asks which channel has the best return on spend. The path data is large and messy, so it feels demanding, but it is rule application. Follow the written procedure literally and the answer falls out, with no analytical judgment in between. The precedence clause makes it worse, since it is exactly the kind of ordering rule the solver only has to execute. To make it deep, drop the policy and ask the business question directly: which channel's budget should be cut to fund a test next quarter. Ship the touch paths alongside a randomized holdout or a staggered market pause for at least one channel. The deciding step becomes estimating incremental return from the experiment, then working out how far the credited figures overstate it for the channels without one.


Quantitative Finance and Trading
Quantitative Finance
Validation the strategy could not have tuned to. The analyst has to decide which evidence actually tests a strategy, given how it was built. A backtest that picked its universe, thresholds and holding period on the same history it reports is a maximum over many tries, not an estimate.

Scenario. A quality-plus-value small cap signal shows an annual alpha of 4.1% (t of 3.2) on 2005 to 2024. The universe is today's index constituents, the fundamentals are the vendor's restated values, and the research log lists 60 variants. A point-in-time constituent history, as-first-reported fundamentals, delisting returns, and a post-launch live record are all in the pack.
What the shallow analysis does. Recomputes the alpha from the research team's portfolio file, adds a transaction cost haircut, runs a subperiod split, and approves. It looks right because every check reproduces the claimed number; none of them leaves the sample the strategy was tuned on.
How to build it. Ship the point-in-time and restated data side by side as a real vendor would, with both clearly labeled, so the issue is evaluation design, not a mislabeled file. Make the deep answer come from three dependent steps: rebuild on point-in-time constituents with delisting returns, discount the in-sample result for the search the log documents, and evaluate on holdout and live periods. Pin it by making the honest alpha land far from the hurdle (for example, t below 1.0 under any reasonable construction) while the shallow alpha sits well above.
Risk that moves with the regime. Volatility clusters, and a risk estimate built on equal-weighted history reacts to a regime shift months late. The analyst has to judge whether a model's misses are random or systematic, and whether a replacement holds up out of sample.

Scenario. A commodity desk sets margin on a calendar spread book using a 2-year historical expected shortfall. The question is whether the margin covered the 2022 energy shock and whether a volatility-scaled version should replace it before winter.
What the shallow analysis does. Counts breaches over the full window, finds them close to the expected rate, and keeps the model. It looks right because the unconditional breach count is the test everyone reports.
How to build it. Give several years of daily P&L with calm stretches and two or three sharp episodes, so breaches bunch after volatility jumps while the total count stays near target. The deep chain: test breach timing, show the lag by rolling the model through history, then choose the replacement's decay on an early window and test it on a later one. Pin it so the timing test rejects decisively and the replacement passes across a reasonable range of decay choices.
Inference that matches how the sample was drawn. Overlapping return windows, persistent predictors, and cross-sectional correlation all make naive standard errors too small. The analyst has to match the inference to the sampling and see what survives.

Scenario. A credit strategist claims the 12-month excess return on high yield after a spread widening above 600 basis points beats cash, using monthly observations of forward 12-month returns since 1990, and wants to make it a standing allocation rule.
What the shallow analysis does. Regresses forward returns on the spread, gets a t above 4 on 400 observations, and adopts the rule. A sharper run corrects for the 11-month overlap and still finds t near 2.3.
How to build it. Pair the overlap with a second issue that is not a single fact: the spread is highly persistent, so the slope estimate is biased upward in a sample this size, and only a handful of independent widening episodes exist. The deep answer needs overlap-aware inference, a correction for the persistent regressor (or a simulation under the null), and an episode-level look. Pin it by making the result fail at 95% under every combination of these, while overlap correction alone still passes, so the one-fact test fails.
Looks deep but is banned. A "research methodology" note in the pack says: rank stocks on 12-month return skipping the last month, form deciles, charge 10 basis points per side, and if turnover exceeds 200% use the 6-month ranking instead. The task asks whether the strategy beats its hurdle. It feels quantitative, but it is rule application: following the note literally produces the answer, and the "if turnover exceeds" clause is a precedence rule no fund would hand a reviewer. To make it deep, delete the note, give the research log and the live record, and make the decision turn on whether the strategy holds up on evidence it was not tuned to.

Banking, Credit & Financial Risk
Seasoning, vintage and calendar in default curves. Loss rates depend on loan age, on the quality of the vintage, and on the economy in the calendar month. These three cannot be separated without some constraint, and the analyst has to find one the data supports and use it to project immature vintages.

Scenario. An auto lender loosened its score cutoff in mid-2024. Management asks whether the loosened vintages are worse or just younger, because the answer decides whether the cutoff stays for 2026.
What the shallow analysis does. Compares current 60-day delinquency or charge-off rates by vintage, finds the 2024 and 2025 vintages lower than 2022, and keeps the cutoff. It looks right because the numbers are real and recent; they are also from loans that have not reached their loss peak.
How to build it. Ship monthly loan-level performance across at least five years of vintages, a regional unemployment series to carry the calendar effect, and booking attributes. The deep chain: build loss by vintage and month on book, separate age from vintage level using the macro series, project immature vintages to lifetime, and compare at matched age. Pin it by making every standard projection (chain ladder on cumulative loss by age, or a hazard model with age and unemployment) put the loosened vintages above the pricing hurdle by a clear margin.
Selection in who you can observe. A scorecard's performance is only observed for applicants the lender approved. Extending a cutoff downward means predicting outcomes for people whose outcomes were never seen, and the analyst has to judge whether the observed relationship carries into that region.

Scenario. A card issuer considers lowering its approval cutoff by 20 points to grow 15%. Two years ago it ran a small random-approval test cell below the cutoff, and that cell's performance is in the pack alongside the full approved book.
What the shallow analysis does. Fits score-to-bad-rate on approved accounts, extrapolates into the new band (about 9% bad), finds the band profitable at that rate, and recommends lowering the cutoff. It looks right because the fit is tight inside the observed range.
How to build it. Make the test cell large enough to estimate the below-cutoff bad rate with useful precision (several thousand accounts), and let the approved book's override approvals in that band be selected on judgment, so they look better than the through-the-door population. The deep chain: recognize the override accounts are selected, estimate the through-the-door bad rate from the random cell (about 15%), reconcile the two, and size profitability. Pin it so the band loses money at any bad rate inside the test cell's confidence interval.
Correlated defaults, not independent ones. Portfolio loss tails come from defaults that move together. The analyst has to measure how much they move together from the history and carry it into the capital or reserve figure.

Scenario. A regional bank's commercial real estate book of 900 loans needs an economic capital figure at 99.9%, and the board will cut new CRE lending if capital exceeds the allocation.
What the shallow analysis does. Multiplies default probability by loss severity by exposure, treats defaults as independent, and gets a tight distribution well inside the allocation. Its stress test at the worst observed year also fits inside. It looks right because the inputs are the bank's own.
How to build it. Provide 15 to 20 years of annual default rates by property type, showing variance far above what independent defaults would produce, and recovery data that worsens in the same years. The deep chain: estimate the common-factor dependence from the excess variance, link severity to the cycle, simulate the tail, compare to the allocation. Pin it by making the tail exceed the allocation for every reasonable dependence estimate from that history, while independence lands well below.
Looks deep but is banned. A task asks for the bank's 90-day default rate on a mortgage book, and the credit policy defines default as 90 days past due or charge-off, "whichever is first, with re-aged accounts treated as current." The answer swings on whether a block of re-aged accounts counts. That is data semantics: one reading of one clause decides it, and telling the solver "re-aged accounts count as defaulted" makes the task routine. To make it deep, keep the definition uncontroversial and make the decision depend on projecting immature vintages or estimating default correlation.

Actuarial Science & Insurance
Immature development and the tail. Recent accident years are mostly unreported or unpaid, and the triangle often stops before claims finish. The analyst has to judge how complete each year is, whether the historical pattern still applies, and how much development lies beyond the last observed age.

Scenario. A general liability insurer's booked reserve is under review after a claims department reorganization. The incurred chain ladder says the reserve is redundant by $8 million, and the CFO wants a release this year.
What the shallow analysis does. Runs the incurred chain ladder with standard averages and no tail, checks it against three-year and five-year averages, and supports the release. It looks right because the variants agree; they share the same diagonal and the same missing tail.
How to build it. Ship claim-level transactions so the analyst can compute average case reserve per open claim by age and see that case adequacy dropped on the latest diagonals while closed-claim severity kept rising. Leave paid development visibly above 1% a year at the last age. The deep chain: detect the adequacy change, restate or switch basis, fit a tail to the later factors, and compare with the booked figure. Pin it so every adjusted projection with a fitted tail shows a deficiency.
Exposure mix and credibility in an indication. A state or class with thin data needs its own experience blended with a complement, and both pieces carry mix effects. The analyst has to restate experience to today's mix and judge how much weight the thin experience deserves, from the data rather than a published standard.

Scenario. A small-state commercial auto program shows a loss ratio of 58% over five years against a permissible 66%, suggesting a cut. The program's fleet size mix shifted heavily toward large fleets, and a regional book of eight states is in the pack as a complement.
What the shallow analysis does. Takes the state's own loss ratio at full weight, or at a weight copied from last year's filing, and files a decrease. It looks right because the state's data is the most direct evidence.
How to build it. Give policy-level data with fleet size, so restating to the current mix is possible and moves the state loss ratio up, and give the regional states enough years to estimate how much true loss ratios differ between states versus within a state over time. The deep chain: restate to current mix, estimate the between-state and within-state variance, weight accordingly, and compare. Pin it so any reasonable variance estimate gives the state less than half weight and the blended indication is an increase.
Trend versus development. When severity jumps on a calendar diagonal (a verdict climate, a medical cost spike), it shows up both as development and as trend. Projecting both double counts it; projecting neither misses it. The analyst has to decide which effect each pattern belongs to and carry it once.

Scenario. A workers' compensation carrier sets its 2026 rate level. Paid severity jumped across all accident years in calendar 2023 and 2024. The pricing team fit severity trend on developed accident-year ultimates and then developed recent years with factors that already include the jump.
What the shallow analysis does. Develops with all-year factors, fits an exponential trend to the ultimates, projects forward, and gets an indication 6 points higher than the deep answer. It looks right because each step is standard and the trend fit is tight.
How to build it. Build incremental paid data with a visible calendar effect across every accident year on the same diagonals. The deep chain: separate calendar-year from development effects (a calendar term in a model of incremental paid, or a separation-type approach), re-derive development without the jump, fit trend to the separated series, then project. Pin it so every specification that carries the calendar effect once lands inside a narrow band and on the other side of the decision line from the double-counted version.
Looks deep but is banned. A homeowners rate review ships a "rate indication manual" that walks through the calculation: trend losses at the selected annual rate, develop with the five-year average factors, divide by on-level premium, compare with the permissible loss ratio, and cap the change at 15%. The task asks for the indicated change. That is rule application: following the manual literally gives the answer, and the cap is a precedence rule. To make it deep, remove the manual, let the trend and development choices be the analyst's to make, and put a mix shift or an immature accident year in the data so those choices decide the indication.

Machine Learning Research
General Statistics & Data Science
In this domain the models are good at the mechanics. They lose when the right number depends on whether the evaluation resembles the decision, whether the observations are as independent as the formula assumes, and whether a ranking reflects the units or the noise. Each strategy below is built so the shallow analysis is internally consistent and still wrong.

011. Validation that leaks through time or through groups
The judgment
Whether the held-out data is separated from the training data the same way the future will be. When rows share a customer, a machine or a patient, or when features are computed from windows that overlap the label, a random split lets the model memorize the unit and the score measures recall of the past.

Scenario
A subscription business wants to replace its rules-based churn list with a classifier, but only if the classifier finds more churners among the 2,000 accounts the retention team can call each month. The data science export has one row per account per month for three years, and the cross-validated AUC is 0.88. Several features are trailing 90-day averages computed at export time.

Why the tempting answer fails
It reuses the random split, maybe reruns it with five folds to show stability, and compares top-2,000 hit rates. The classifier wins by a wide margin. The stability check passes because every fold leaks the same way.

How to build it
Ship the monthly panel, the feature definitions as code and the rules list's historical call outcomes. Make the leak structural: account months appear on both sides of a random split, and the trailing windows reach past the prediction date. A forward split grouped by account, with features rebuilt as of each prediction date, drops the AUC to about 0.71, and at 2,000 calls a month the classifier's lead shrinks to a statistical tie or reverses. Pin the decision in the prompt with the calling capacity and "more churners reached next quarter", so once the evaluation is honest only one answer follows. The deep step is not spotting the leak, which strong runs often do. It is rebuilding the features point in time and then judging the result at the operating capacity with an interval that respects repeated accounts.

022. A held-out set that does not look like deployment
The judgment
Whether performance measured on one population carries to the population the decision will act on, and how to reweight or restrict when it does not.

Scenario
A lender's approval model was validated on applications from its branch channel. The decision is whether to switch it on for a new online partner, where applicants are younger, have thinner credit files and come in through different marketing. The partner sent six months of applications with outcomes for the small share approved under the old rules.

Why the tempting answer fails
It scores the partner applications, sees an AUC close to the branch figure on the approved subset and concludes the model transfers. The check passes because the approved subset looks like the branch population; that is why it was approved.

How to build it
Ship the branch validation data, the partner's full application file (large, with thin-file applicants making up a third of it) and outcomes only for approved partner applicants. Make the model's error concentrate in thin files, which branch data barely covers. The deep chain is recognizing that the outcome sample is selected on the old rules, estimating performance by segment of file depth where outcomes exist, reweighting to the partner's full applicant mix, and carrying the gap in expected loss at the approval threshold to the go or no-go. Pin the decision with a loss ceiling per approved dollar. Make sure the thin-file segment has enough labeled outcomes to estimate its loss rate tightly, so the conclusion does not depend on an extrapolation choice.

033. Calibration and discrimination when the decision uses a threshold
The judgment
Whether a better ranking makes better decisions when the business acts on a fixed probability cutoff. A model can separate classes better and still assign probabilities that sit on the wrong side of the line.

Scenario
A claims team auto-approves any claim whose predicted probability of being fraudulent is under 2%, a cutoff set from the cost of an investigation against the average loss on a missed fraud. A retrained model raises AUC from 0.81 to 0.85. It was trained on a rebalanced sample, and the fraud rate in recent months is lower than in the training period.

Why the tempting answer fails
It reports the higher AUC and better precision at a fixed top-k, then recommends the new model. Both statements are true, and neither is the decision, because the business uses the 2% line, not a top-k.

How to build it
Ship the training sample, the training config as it would really exist (with the rebalancing setting in it, not called out), the last six months of scored claims with investigation outcomes, and the investigation cost table. At the 2% cut the new model auto-approves about 30% more claims, and most of the extra approvals are fraud, so expected cost rises. The deep chain is checking calibration on recent outcomes, recalibrating for the current fraud rate, re-deriving the cut from the cost ratio, and then comparing expected cost per claim with an interval. Build the data so the recalibrated new model is clearly better, or clearly not, by a margin that survives any reasonable calibration method. The prompt pins the costs and asks which model the team should run. It never mentions calibration.

044. Variance components and shrinkage when ranking many small units
The judgment
How much of the spread between units is real and how much is sampling noise, and what that does to a ranking that drives an action.

Scenario
A distributor will drop the five worst of its 48 suppliers by defect rate. Volumes range from 40 lots a year to 3,000. The raw ranking puts small suppliers at both the top and the bottom, and lots from the same supplier are correlated because they share a production run.

Why the tempting answer fails
It ranks raw defect rates, perhaps after dropping suppliers below a volume cutoff it picks itself, and names five. The list looks sensible because the bottom suppliers do have bad numbers.

How to build it
Ship lot-level inspection records (large, with lot splits and re-inspections that have to be resolved), supplier volume history and the contract terms. Generate defects with a real between-supplier variance component and lot-level overdispersion, so a binomial model understates uncertainty. The deep chain is estimating the between-supplier variance with overdispersion allowed, shrinking each supplier toward the mean by its effective sample size, ranking on the shrunken rates, and checking how many of the five are distinguishable from the sixth. Pin it by making the shrunken bottom five clearly separated from the rest, with two of the raw bottom five being small suppliers that shrink back to the middle. Any self-chosen volume cutoff then gives a different, wrong list. This is also a multiple-comparisons problem: 48 noisy rates guarantee some extreme ones, which is exactly why a raw list passes a sniff test.

055. Dependence that breaks independent standard errors
The judgment
Whether the unit of analysis matches the unit of assignment, and how much the effective sample shrinks when it does not.

Scenario
A warehouse tested a new pick-path method on 6 of its 12 shifts for eight weeks and logged 380,000 individual picks. The rollout costs money, and the operations director will approve it only if the time saving per pick is established at conventional confidence and worth at least 0.4 seconds.

Why the tempting answer fails
It runs a regression on picks with a treatment dummy and gets a 0.6-second saving with a tiny standard error. It may even add picker fixed effects and report that the result holds up with controls. The controls do not touch the problem: assignment was by shift, so there are 12 independent units, not 380,000.

How to build it
Ship the pick log (large, with scanner gaps and multi-line orders), the shift roster, the treatment schedule and the order-mix file. Give shifts real heterogeneity: night shifts pick a different order mix and one treated shift had a staffing change midway. The deep chain is recognizing the shift as the unit, adjusting for order mix where it differs by shift, and computing the effect with shift-level uncertainty (cluster-robust with a small-cluster correction, or a shift-level comparison), which puts the lower bound below 0.4 seconds. Pin it with the 0.4-second bar and a design where every reasonable shift-level analysis gives the same verdict. Simpson-type mix effects belong here too: if treated shifts carried more single-line orders, the pooled saving can exceed the saving within every order type, or even reverse its sign.

Looks deep but is banned
06Example A: the duplicate images.
Why this is banned
A task asks whether a defect-detection model is ready to deploy. The deciding fact is that 300 test images also appear in the training set, listed in a hashes file in the input pack. Find the overlap, drop it, and the accuracy falls below the bar. This is a planted defect and a single-fact stump: tell the solver "some test images are duplicates" and the rest is routine. To make it deep, let the overlap come from structure that a real pipeline creates (several photos of the same physical part taken on different days, with part IDs in the metadata), so the solver has to choose a grouped evaluation itself. Then make the deployment decision depend on performance at a fixed false-reject rate on the production line's defect mix, which differs from the test set's, so the honest evaluation still has to be carried to the operating point and reweighted before the answer is clear.

07Example B: the validation standard.
Why this is banned
The pack includes a "Model Risk Validation Standard" that says to compute a population stability index for each feature, retrain if any exceeds 0.25, and if two features exceed 0.10 use the stricter of the two remediation paths. The runs compute PSI correctly and still miss because they apply the precedence clause the other way. This is rule application, with a data-reading stump on top: the procedure is written out, and the answer depends on how one clause is read. To make it deep, drop the rulebook. Pin only the business outcome, such as "retrain if expected loss at the current approval threshold has risen by more than 15 basis points", and let the data show a shift in a few features that matters for loss only in one applicant segment. The solver then has to estimate the shift's effect on the decision by reweighting recent applicants against the development sample, not tick a threshold on a drift statistic.

Nowcasting through a reporting delay that has changed. The judgment is that recent counts are incomplete, and that the delay distribution used to correct them has to come from the current reporting pattern, not from past seasons. Scenario: a county is about to end an outbreak response because cases by onset week have fallen below its threshold. The shallow analysis inflates recent weeks using last season's delay distribution, or ignores the delay entirely, and approves the stand-down. It looks right because a backtest on last season validates the correction, and last season's delays really were short, so the check has no way to see the change. Excess mortality that has to be justified out of sample. The judgment is that an excess-death estimate is only as good as its baseline, and the baseline has to be chosen by how well it predicts held-out periods, then carried through with overdispersion and any later deficit. Scenario: a county files for state reimbursement after a heat wave, and the reimbursement counts deaths that would not otherwise have occurred that summer. The shallow analysis subtracts a five-year average for the same weeks and reports a large excess. It looks right because the peak lines up with the temperatures and the death files reconcile with annual totals. Scenario: a city installs gunshot detection sensors in the 12 neighborhoods with the most shootings last year. Shootings there fall 31 percent the next year, and the police department wants to buy sensors for 12 more neighborhoods.
What the shallow analysis does: compares sensor neighborhoods with all others over the same years, finds a large relative drop, and checks that the trends were parallel over the prior five years. On a five-year view they mostly were.
Build it: publish the ranking used for selection, make neighborhoods ranked 13 to 24 fall almost as much without sensors, and make neighborhoods matched on several prior years give the same small effect, below the purchase bar.

Engineering and Physical Sciences
Physical & Environmental Sciences
Censoring at shifting detection limits. Nondetects are not zeros and not half the limit: they are left-censored values, and when the reporting limit changes over time, substitution manufactures trends and exceedance rates that are not in the water, soil or tissue.

Scenario: a state wants to know whether mercury in walleye from a reservoir has fallen enough since a power plant closed to lift a consumption advisory. Over 20 years the lab's reporting limit fell from 0.10 to 0.02 mg/kg, and early samples are half nondetects.
What the shallow analysis does: substitutes half the reporting limit, fits a trend on log concentration and finds a steep, significant decline. It looks right because substitution is common practice and the decline matches the story everyone expects. Part of that decline is the falling limit itself, since early nondetects were assigned values five times higher than later ones.
How to build it: give the series a real but modest decline that only a censored method recovers (maximum likelihood with a censored lognormal, Kaplan-Meier, or a censored regression with fish length as a covariate). Make the advisory threshold sit between the substitution answer and the censored answer, and check that every proper censored estimator lands on the same side. Ship the lab method sheets that document the limits, so the information is available, but never say what to do with it.
Serial dependence and change points in sensor series. High-frequency sensor data carry strong autocorrelation, so tests that treat each reading as independent find significance almost anywhere, and the date a change began is an empirical question, not the date an intervention started.

Scenario: a turbidity sonde below a highway construction site reports every 10 minutes, and the permit penalizes the contractor if turbidity rose because of the work. A clear rise appears after grading began.
What the shallow analysis does: compares means before and after the grading start date with a t-test on daily values and finds p below 0.001. It looks right because the rise is real and visible on any chart. It misses that the rise began three weeks earlier with a wet spell, that an upstream sonde shows the same rise, and that with lag-one autocorrelation near 0.9 the effective sample size is a small fraction of the nominal one.
How to build it: include an upstream reference sonde and a rainfall record. Build the downstream-minus-upstream difference so it has no break at the grading date, and set the autocorrelation high enough that any dependence-aware method (AR errors, prewhitening, block bootstrap, weekly aggregation) gives an interval spanning zero while the naive interval does not. Pin the decision with the permit's "attributable to the work" language and a significance requirement.
Spatial dependence and preferential sampling in monitoring networks. Monitors and wells are rarely placed at random. When they cluster near sources or towns, the raw average overstates the area-wide value, and neighboring stations carry overlapping information, so the network knows less than its station count suggests.

Scenario: a groundwater district must report whether average nitrate across an agricultural basin exceeds a planning level that triggers a fertilizer program. Two thirds of the 140 sampled wells are municipal and domestic wells clustered around three towns downgradient of dairies.
What the shallow analysis does: averages all wells and computes a t-based upper bound. It looks right because the sample is large and the method is textbook. The clusters drag the mean up and the independence assumption shrinks the interval.
How to build it: generate nitrate from a spatial field with a variogram range of a few kilometers and place wells preferentially in the high zones. Make declustered estimates (cell declustering, Voronoi weights or block kriging) agree with each other and fall on the opposite side of the planning level from the raw mean. Ship well coordinates, construction depths and a land-use layer so the spatial structure can be shown from the data, and keep at least one irrelevant layer (soil series, say) as a distractor.
Looks deep but is banned. A sampling task ships a quality assurance plan that says to substitute half the detection limit for nondetects, average field duplicates, then compare the 95th percentile with the standard. The task feels technical, but a solver who follows the plan literally gets the answer with no judgment: that is rule application. To make it deep, remove the procedure, state only the decision and the threshold, and let the data force a censored estimator whose result differs from substitution.

Energy Systems & Utilities
Weather normalization and the wrong baseline in savings verification. Normalizing for weather is necessary but rarely sufficient. The baseline period and the counterfactual decide most savings estimates, and programs that recruit customers after a high bill have a built-in regression to the mean.

Scenario: a utility claims 11 percent savings from a residential heat-pump water heater rebate and needs to clear 7 percent for a shareholder incentive. Customers applied after their old water heater failed, often after months of rising consumption from a degraded unit.
What the shallow analysis does: fits heating and cooling degree-day models at a fixed 65 degree balance point to the 12 months before and after installation and reports 11 percent. It looks right because weather is handled, the model fits well and the method is what most evaluators do first.
How to build it: give participants a visible pre-installation run-up relative to their own multi-year history, and give matched nonparticipants a background decline from codes and prices over the same window. Let building-specific balance points differ enough that a fixed 65 degrees biases the weather adjustment. Make the multi-year baseline estimate and the matched comparison estimate agree with each other and fall below the incentive line. Pin "savings" in the prompt as what the program caused, never as a named method.
Peak coincidence and load diversity in capacity planning. Equipment is sized to the coincident peak of everything it serves, not to the sum of each customer's own peak and not to a new load's average. Diversity cuts the sum; coincidence on design days can push it back up.

Scenario: a cooperative must decide whether a feeder can absorb 300 new residential EV chargers in a subdivision without a reconductoring project. Each charger is 11 kW.
What the shallow analysis does: either multiplies 300 by 11 kW and declares an overload, or applies an average coincidence factor from national studies and declares plenty of room. Both look defensible because each cites a real number.
How to build it: ship interval data from a nearby subdivision with EV owners, so the analyst can estimate coincident charging demand at the feeder's peak hour, and make that peak hour shift on hot weekday evenings when commuters plug in at once. Let the empirically estimated coincident draw on design days sit between the two shallow answers and decide the reconductoring question. Pin the feeder rating and the planning criterion (for example, a 1-in-10 summer peak) in the prompt.
Extreme tails versus normal assumptions. Planning criteria talk about rare events: a 1-in-10 peak, a once-a-decade wind lull, a hot spot that degrades a transformer. Tails of weather-driven loads are rarely normal, and a few years of data almost never contain the design event.

Scenario: a grid operator must set how much firm backup to hold for a region where wind supplies a third of winter energy, sized to the longest multi-day low-wind spell expected once in 20 years.
What the shallow analysis does: fits a normal distribution to annual minimum three-day wind output from 8 years of plant data and reads off the 1-in-20 level. It looks right because it uses the operator's own fleet data and gives a precise number.
How to build it: pair the 8 years of plant output with 45 years of reanalysis wind speeds and a power curve, so the analyst can reconstruct a long synthetic record and fit an extreme-value distribution (block maxima or peaks over threshold) with a sound out-of-sample check. Make the long-record tail heavier than the normal fit so the backup requirement moves across the threshold that decides whether a new peaker is needed. Check that GEV and peaks-over-threshold fits agree on the side.
Looks deep but is banned. A capacity task ranks five candidate substations by headroom and says that if the top site would exceed its transformer cap, the load goes to the next. The arithmetic can be long, but the decision is a precedence rule spelled out for the solver: rule application. To make it deep, require the analyst to estimate each site's 1-in-10 coincident peak from weather and interval data, so the ranking itself is the output of a modeling judgment and a shallow peak estimate would reorder the sites.

Human Factors & Ergonomics
Learning and order effects in usability trials. Participants get better with practice and carry strategies from one condition to the next. In a crossover trial, unbalanced order or asymmetric carryover can produce a design difference that is really a session difference.

Scenario: a rail operator compares two dispatcher screens in a simulator, each dispatcher using both. Scheduling conflicts left most dispatchers on the old screen first, and the new screen shows fewer missed conflicts.
What the shallow analysis does: runs a paired comparison of each dispatcher's error rates under the two screens and finds a significant advantage for the new one. It looks right because the pairing is respected and the effect size is plausible.
How to build it: give the data a strong period effect (second sessions better regardless of screen) and an order imbalance large enough that the paired estimate is mostly practice. Include enough dispatchers in each order that a mixed model with period and sequence terms can separate the effects cleanly, and set the period-adjusted difference on the other side of the operator's adoption bar from the paired estimate. If you add carryover, make it detectable from the sequence-by-period pattern, not something the solver has to assume. A practice log or a warm-up block in each session gives the analyst a second, independent view of the learning curve, which helps pin the answer.
Repeated measures analyzed as independent. Trials are nested in participants, often crossed with stimuli. Treating thousands of trials as independent inflates certainty and lets a few participants or a few hard items drive the result.

Scenario: an automaker tests two dashboard layouts for glance time, with 24 drivers each making 400 glances across 20 menu tasks. The safety team approves a layout only if it cuts long glances (over 2 seconds) with 95 percent confidence.
What the shallow analysis does: runs a chi-square test on 9,600 glances and finds a highly significant reduction. It looks right because the counts are large and the test is standard.
How to build it: make the reduction come mostly from four drivers and two menu tasks, so a model with crossed random effects for driver and task gives an interval that spans zero. Make the driver-level and task-level variance obvious in the data (for example, some drivers rarely glance long under either layout). Pin the confidence requirement in the prompt and let the design dictate the unit of analysis. Check that driver-clustered bootstrap intervals and the mixed model agree, so the deep answer does not depend on which dependence-aware method the solver picks.
Speed-accuracy tradeoffs that flip which design is better. A design that is more accurate when people take their time can be worse at the pace the job actually runs. Self-paced trials let participants trade time for accuracy, so the raw comparison answers the wrong question when the operational pace is fixed.

Scenario: a parcel hub compares two label-scanning workflows for sorters. The trial was self-paced; on the floor, the belt moves at a fixed rate that gives each parcel about 3 seconds.
What the shallow analysis does: compares raw mis-sort rates and picks the workflow with fewer errors, which is also the slower one. It looks right because accuracy is what the hub cares about and the difference is significant.
How to build it: give each workflow a different error-versus-time curve, with the more accurate workflow degrading faster when time is short, and make sure trial handling times span the operational budget for both so the answer is an interpolation. Include item difficulty (damaged labels) that lengthens handling time and raises errors, so a model without item or participant effects bends the curve. State the belt pace in the prompt as the business constraint, never as a hint about method.
Looks deep but is banned. A usability task hinges on whether a self-corrected slip counts as an error under the study's coding manual: count it and design A wins, skip it and design B wins. That is a data-semantics reading, and if the manual is ambiguous it is also unpinned. To make it deep, pin the error definition plainly in the prompt and move the deciding step to something the data must settle, such as the order effect or the speed-accuracy curve at the operational pace.

Other
Survey Research & Official Statistics
Survey tasks go deep when the answer depends on how the sample was drawn and who answered, not on the arithmetic of a weighted mean. The models know the formulas. They lose when they apply a formula that ignores the design, the missingness or the nonresponse mechanism the data reveals.

011. Combine multiple imputation with the survey design
Why this is banned
The judgment: When an outcome item has heavy nonresponse, the imputation model has to carry the design (strata, clusters, and the variables that drive the weights) and the predictors of missingness. The variance has to add the between-imputation part to the design-based within-imputation part.
Scenario: A state office must say whether a county's child poverty rate exceeds 25 percent, the line for a supplemental nutrition grant. Household income is missing for 23 percent of respondents, and it is missing far more often for self-employed and multi-earner households, which in this county skew lower-income because of seasonal farm work.
What the shallow analysis does: A weighted complete-case estimate, or a single hot-deck imputation within age by tenure cells, gives about 22.6 percent with a tight replicate standard error. It reproduces the published rate for respondents with income, so it looks validated, and the single imputation hides how much the missing values could move the answer.
Build it so the deep step is real: Ship the observed predictors of missingness (self-employment, number of earners, benefit receipt from linked records) so that the data support missing at random given those variables, along with replicate weights. Set the county so that a design-consistent multiple imputation with 20 or more draws lands near 26.5 percent. Check that every imputation model including the missingness predictors gives the same side of 25, and that dropping them is what moves the answer. That check is what pins it.
022. Small-area estimates that borrow strength honestly
Why this is banned
The judgment: Direct estimates for small domains are too noisy to rank. A Fay-Herriot style model shrinks each one toward a regression prediction from auxiliary data in proportion to its noise, and the sampling variances themselves usually need smoothing because they are estimated from a handful of cases.
Scenario: A health department will fund outreach in the ten counties with the highest uninsured rates from a statewide survey of 11,000 adults. Thirty of the 77 counties have fewer than 60 respondents. Two of those have direct estimates above 30 percent, and one has a direct estimate of zero with an estimated variance of zero.
What the shallow analysis does: It ranks the direct estimates, or it shrinks every county toward the state mean without covariates. The first rewards noise; the second pulls genuinely high-uninsured rural counties toward the middle. Both pass the obvious check, because the population-weighted average of county estimates still matches the state total.
Build it so the deep step is real: Ship strong county covariates with documented sources (Medicaid enrollment rates, the share of jobs in small firms, a prior census-based estimate), the direct estimates with their design variances, and enough counties that the model variance is well estimated. Arrange the data so the model-based top ten differ from the direct top ten by three or four counties. Then confirm that the top ten hold across reasonable covariate sets and variance smoothers, so the list is pinned and not a modeling preference.
033. Nonresponse bias that weighting does not remove
Why this is banned
The judgment: Weighting on frame variables fixes bias only when response depends on those variables. If response is tied to the outcome within weighting classes, the analyst has to find the evidence (an auxiliary variable known for respondents and nonrespondents alike that tracks the outcome) and calibrate on it or model response with it.
Scenario: A quarterly job vacancy survey of 4,000 establishments has a 48 percent response rate, lower among growing firms because their hiring managers are busy. The office weights within size by industry classes and reports 182,000 openings, under the 200,000 that triggers a state training appropriation. Unemployment insurance wage records, linked for every sampled establishment, show each one's employment change over the quarter.
What the shallow analysis does: It weights by size and industry, then checks that weighted employment matches the administrative total within 1 percent. The check passes because employment level is built into the weighting classes; the outcome tracks employment change, which is not.
Build it so the deep step is real: Make the linked employment change available for respondents and nonrespondents, make it strongly correlated with vacancies among respondents, and make respondents' growth visibly lower than nonrespondents' within every weighting class. Calibrating on employment change, or a response model that includes it, should raise openings by 10 to 15 percent and clear the trigger. Make sure the conclusion holds whether the analyst calibrates or models response propensity, so the method choice between them does not decide it.
04Looks deep but is banned: a weighting manual that does the work.
Why this is banned
The input pack includes a methodology manual giving the weighting steps: compute nonresponse factors by cell, collapse any cell with fewer than 20 respondents into the next cell in a listed order, trim at 3.5 times the median weight, then rake to four margins. The task is decided by whether the solver follows the collapse order. That is rule application: following the written procedure literally produces the answer, however messy the files. To make it deep, drop the procedure and ship the evidence instead: response propensities that differ on variables beyond the frame cells, and an auxiliary variable that tracks the outcome, so the analyst has to decide from the data which adjustment removes the bias and show that the simpler one does not.

Urban & Regional Science
Urban tasks go deep when places affect each other and when the unit of analysis changes the answer. A neighborhood next to a program is rarely untouched by it, and a number computed over zones is rarely the number for the people living in them.

051. Spillover and displacement around place-based investments
Why this is banned
The judgment: A program in one place can pull activity from nearby places. Nearby areas then make a contaminated comparison, and the net effect is the treated gain minus what was taken from the surroundings.
Scenario: A region's new light-rail line opened six stations in 2021 with a density bonus within half a mile. Permitted housing units in the station areas rose 2,900 above trend. The corridor's short-run housing demand is close to fixed, and permits in the ring from half a mile to two miles fell over the same years.
What the shallow analysis does: A difference-in-differences against the adjacent ring. The ring shares market conditions, its pre-period path tracks the station areas closely, and the effect looks large and clean. But the ring's decline is part of what the program did, so the estimate counts the displaced units twice.
Build it so the deep step is real: Give parcel-level permits with dates and coordinates across the whole region, so the shift in permits with distance is visible, and include distant comparable corridors with matching pre-trends. Set the net regional gain well under half of the gross figure, and pin the decision on net new units, for example whether the bonus meets a units-per-dollar target for extending it to the next line. Confirm the conclusion holds across any reasonable ring radius.
062. The modifiable areal unit problem when the question is about people
Why this is banned
The judgment: Averages over zones depend on how the zones are drawn. When the question counts people (how many residents lack access), the estimate has to be built at the finest level the data allows and then summed, and the analyst has to show why the zone-level answer is biased in this geography.
Scenario: A transit agency's equity grant counts residents who cannot reach 50,000 jobs within 45 minutes by transit. At the tract level, using tract centroids and tract populations, 61,000 residents fall short. On the region's edge, tracts mix dense apartment clusters near an arterial with large, sparse blocks far from any stop.
What the shallow analysis does: It computes travel times from tract centroids, applies the threshold to each tract and sums populations. It cross-checks with block groups, gets a similar number, and concludes the zoning does not matter. The cross-check feels like a sensitivity test, but block groups on the edge share the same centroid problem.
Build it so the deep step is real: Ship block-level population, a fine travel-time matrix and the street network, and place the dense clusters so centroid-based times misstate access for most of a tract's residents. The block-based count (say 88,000) should cross the grant's eligibility threshold while the tract and block-group answers do not. The prompt pins the quantity as a count of residents, which makes the person-level build the only correct one.
073. Net, not gross, regional economic impact
Why this is banned
The judgment: Input-output multipliers apply to new final demand in the region. Spending that residents would have made anyway, or that leaks to outside suppliers, is not new demand, and retail spending has to enter at its local margin, not its sticker price.
Scenario: A county is asked for $60 million toward an arena on a projected $410 million a year in economic impact, computed as gross attendee spending times a Type II multiplier of 1.9. The county's standing test is that public money must return at least $1.50 in new regional output per dollar over 20 years.
What the shallow analysis does: It uses the regional multiplier table correctly, cleans the attendance projections, and applies a modest 10 percent substitution haircut taken from a published study. The return clears $1.50 comfortably, and the haircut makes the analysis look skeptical.
Build it so the deep step is real: Ship a ticketing file with buyer ZIP codes showing that 72 percent of attendees live in the region, consumer spending data showing entertainment spending is a stable share of resident income, concession and merchandise contracts with out-of-region operators, and input-output tables with retail margins. Netting out resident substitution and leakage, and applying the multiplier only to local value added, should take the return to about $0.60 per dollar. Check that it stays below $1.50 under any reasonable substitution share between 60 and 90 percent of resident spending.
08Looks deep but is banned: a headway field that decides everything.
Why this is banned
A bus reliability task hinges on whether the agency's headway field records scheduled or observed gaps between buses: read one way, the route fails the reliability threshold; read the other, it passes. That is a data semantics stump, however much modeling surrounds it, and the one-fact test confirms it (tell the solver which reading is right and the task is routine). To make it deep, say in the prompt that reliability means observed headways, and move the decision to a judgment the data has to support, for example whether ridership fell because of reliability or because of a fare change that happened the same quarter, separated using routes that got one change but not the other.

Education & Learning Sciences
Education tasks go deep when the signal the district acts on is mostly noise, selection or rebound. Test scores are measured with error, schools are small, cohorts differ, and programs go to schools that were chosen for a reason. The shallow analysis takes the score at face value.

091. Reliability-adjusted school effects with cohort-to-cohort variation
Why this is banned
The judgment: A school's growth estimate mixes a persistent school effect, a school-by-year shock (one unusual cohort, a teacher leaving midyear) and student sampling error. Shrinkage that counts only sampling error over-trusts single years, especially for small schools.
Scenario: A district gives a $150,000 recognition award to its five highest-growth middle schools, meant to reward sustained growth. This year four of the five are small schools, and two of them were below the district median the year before.
What the shallow analysis does: It applies empirical Bayes shrinkage with the standard errors from this year's model. The small schools move down slightly and stay in the top five. Its check, that shrunk estimates correlate highly with raw ones and that large schools barely move, is exactly what shrinkage should show, so it looks right.
Build it so the deep step is real: Ship four or five years of student-level results so the school-by-year variance can be estimated from the data, and make it about as large as the persistent variance. With multi-year weighting and both error sources, three of the five award schools should drop out. The prompt pins the purpose (sustained growth, not one year); check that the top five hold across reasonable variance estimators.
102. Measurement error in value-added
Why this is banned
The judgment: Prior test scores are measured with error, and the error is largest at the extremes of the scale. Regressing current scores on noisy priors flattens the prior-score coefficient, which biases value-added against teachers whose students start far from the middle.
Scenario: A district places the bottom 10 percent of teachers on value-added into mandatory coaching. Teachers of newcomer English learners, whose students start near the scale floor, make up a quarter of the list.
What the shallow analysis does: It fits the standard model with one prior score and demographic controls, adds classroom composition controls, and finds the newcomer teachers still at the bottom. Adding controls feels like the right test for bias, the list barely changes, and the analysis reports the list as stable.
Build it so the deep step is real: Ship two or three years of prior scores per student and the test publisher's technical report with conditional standard errors by scale score, so the correction can be done either by an errors-in-variables adjustment or by using one prior year as an instrument for another. The correction should move most of the newcomer teachers off the list. Pin the decision on list membership and confirm that both correction routes agree, so neither choice decides it.
113. Regression to the mean when schools are picked for low scores
Why this is banned
The judgment: Schools chosen because of one low year rebound on average with no intervention at all. The counterfactual for a turnaround program has to come from schools selected the same way in years without the program, or from schools just above the selection cutoff.
Scenario: The 15 lowest-scoring elementary schools in 2022 received a turnaround grant. Their proficiency rose 9 points in 2023 and 11 in 2024, against 2 for the district. The board is deciding whether to renew at $4 million a year, and its renewal line is a 5-point gain attributable to the grant.
What the shallow analysis does: It compares the grant schools' gains with the district's, or with schools of similar demographics, and finds a large effect. Matching on demographics feels rigorous, but the matched schools were not selected on a bad year, so the comparison still carries the rebound.
Build it so the deep step is real: Ship school-level results back to 2015 and the ranking variable used for selection, so the analyst can see that the bottom 15 schools of 2017, 2018 and 2019 also gained 6 to 8 points over two years with no program, and so that schools just above the 2022 cutoff can serve as a second comparison. Both approaches should put the effect near 2 points, under the renewal line, which is what pins the answer.
12Looks deep but is banned: a district standard that ranks the schools.
Why this is banned
The input pack includes a district accountability standard that says to rank schools on a three-year weighted growth index (weights 0.5, 0.3 and 0.2), break ties on the share of students proficient, and then on enrollment. The task asks which school goes into the intervention tier. Applying the standard literally gives the answer, so it is rule application, even with messy student files underneath. To make it deep, supply no ranking formula: state what the tier is for (schools whose effect on growth is persistently lowest) and let the analyst justify a variance-components model and shrinkage from the data, with the resulting list stable across reasonable choices.

Strategies, not templates
None of the strategies names a dataset or a scenario, on purpose. Pick one that fits your data and build your own version. Two tasks with the same strategy on the same data structure are treated as duplicates.

How to use a strategy
Build the decision so that the shallow analysis and the deep one land on different answers, and the data's own structure rules out every other defensible route. The prompt pins only the business question, the decision it feeds, the outcome or threshold that decides it, and the deliverables. It never names the method, the comparison group, the confounder or the adjustment. The strategy decides the main recommendation; it is not a side ask.
