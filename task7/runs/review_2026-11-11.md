## The review (returned)

What's good:
This is a serious piece of Project Sulfur work, and a lot of it holds. Haldenau's 2026 grant round is a real energy
efficiency evaluation question: the pilot's randomised draw in pilot_2019_responses_and_draw.xlsx gives a clean
comparison of works buildings against the owners the draw passed over, gas_meter_reads_2013_2026.csv has to be rebuilt
season by season through meter exchanges, rollovers, corrections and off-date reads before it can be weather
normalised under benchmarking_method_2022.pdf, and the savings then have to be carried onto this year's applications
along genuine heterogeneity (controls by the share of the week a building stands empty from
electricity_load_profiles_2025.csv, insulation by roof per floor from solar_cadastre_rooftops_2024.xlsx). Heating
optimisation's decaying saving over its 15-year life, so that the seven-season average would wrongly fund it, is a
good modelling judgment. The domain fit is right: this is what retrofit evaluation in an energy utility setting
actually asks.

Determinism and correctness hold. The committed answer (fund insulation and heating controls, not heating
optimisation) reproduces from the shipped data files: 829 live applications asking EUR 4,959,800 once revisions
replace earlier applications, flip saving rates of 20.34%, 3.18% and 7.44%, about 294, 692 and 459 applications at the
30 June close, and grant per tonne of about EUR 108.69, 193.18 and 103.25 against the EUR 120 limit, each 90% range on
its own side of the limit. Section 4 of grant_offer_2026_terms.pdf ("works not completed by then receives nothing")
together with the 2019 job durations tracking roof area pins the payout reading, so the single answer is supported.

What's bad:
The task is not being returned for correctness or determinism. It is being returned because the step that decides the
answer is a single hidden factor built into the data, which is one of the overused shapes Project Sulfur no longer
accepts as the deciding step. It also turned out not difficult enough: the test responses reproduced the pilot
estimates, the empty-week and roof-per-floor transports, the decay, the close counts and every carbon call, so all of
the genuine analysis was solved.

The only place the test responses left the committed answer was insulation's payout by 30 September. The hidden factor
is roofer capacity: only 12 of the 26 roofers who did the 2019 roofs quote this year, and this year's roofs average
1,121 square metres against 802 in 2019, so at their 2019 pace in square metres a working day only about 172 of the 275
insulation roofs that go ahead can finish. That cuts insulation's payout from about EUR 8.27 million to EUR 5,496,863
and brings controls plus insulation under the EUR 8.2 million budget. The factor was constructed into the data and
bites only in insulation (the heating firms have room), the one measure whose payout decides the budget step. The test
responses never looked at it; they said "Historical arrivals and completion remain informative" and concluded "its
expected payment is EUR 8,273,175, above the whole budget", funding controls alone.

Once a solver is told in one sentence that the roofers quoting this year cannot finish this year's larger roofs by 30
September at their 2019 pace, the rest is one routine adjustment: pace times working days per firm. Your own note says
every reasonable reading of that step lands within about a tenth. The "find the one hidden driver" lesson is the most
repeated shape in recent submissions, and it teaches a model little.

The standard in short: (1) really use the assigned domain, so that swapping the nouns would break the task (you do this
well); (2) two layers, realistic messy data plus analytical or methodological rigor that decides the answer; (3)
planted defects, decoys and hidden rules, data semantics, rule application and one hidden factor may appear, but cannot
be the deciding step. The test to apply: could a strong analyst who cleaned the data and read every rule correctly
still get the answer wrong by doing the analysis the easy way? Here the answer is no, because the easy-way analysis
already gets every carbon call right and only the capacity factor separates the outcomes.

What needs fixing:
- The deciding step (insulation's payout under roofer capacity) is one constructed hidden factor whose fix is routine
  once named; move the decision onto analysis.
- The carbon calls, which carry the real analytical work, are on a wide enough margin that the test responses reached
  all three; make at least one of them depend on a judgment that the easy route gets wrong.
- If capacity stays in the task, state it in a shipped file (for example in grant_offer_2026_terms.pdf or
  committee_request_2026-06-15.docx: "works paid only for roofs completed by 30 September by the contractors quoting;
  plan on each firm's 2019 square metres per working day") so it is part of the setup rather than the trap.

How to fix it:
Keep the data package, the pilot, the meter work and the determinism. Then let the funding call turn on one or more of
these analytical steps instead of on the roofer count, each asked for in the prompt with its estimand stated:
1. Completion as a forecast, not a hidden cap: ask for insulation's expected payout by 30 September as a projection of
   firm-level completion times from the 2019 jobs (durations against roof area, start dates, firm workloads), with a
   90% band, and set the budget so the call depends on whether the funded pair's payout band clears EUR 8.2 million.
   The judgment is then how to model completion and its uncertainty, not whether to notice capacity at all.
2. Uncertainty that matches the design: the draw was made by lot within each measure, and buildings sit within owners
   and use types. Ask for each grant per tonne with a range that resamples at the level the draw and the clustering
   imply, and move the limit or the budget so that a naive building-level range puts controls or insulation on the
   wrong side of EUR 120 while the design-based range does not.
3. Separating two explanations for heating optimisation's fall: ask for the life saving with the decay separated from
   the 2022 price shock and the service team's burner checks that also hit the passed-over owners, so the call on
   heating optimisation depends on how that decomposition is done rather than on a fitted curve alone.
An answer shape other than an effect compared with a cutoff also fits this data well: an allocation of the EUR 8.2
million across measures and late applications that maximises lifetime tonnes, or a bridge from grant asked (EUR
11,451,661) to grant paid broken into withdrawals, late arrivals and unfinished works.

Ways to make the task harder:
1. Bring the controls call closer to the limit (this year's empty-week share of 68.15% against the pilot's 38.38% is a
   long transport) so that a straight-line transport through the round-the-clock hotels and care homes funds or drops
   controls differently from a model that treats them as their own band, and ask for the transported rate with its
   range.
2. Make the late-arrival projection matter: ask for applications and grant at close with a band from the 2019 arrival
   curves, and set the budget so the funded set changes between a measure-by-measure arrival model and a pooled one.
3. Tie insulation's saving to the cadastre with measurement error (roof faces summed at addresses with variant street
   spellings) so that the roof-per-floor slope is attenuated unless the solver corrects for it, and let that correction
   move insulation's grant per tonne across EUR 120.
4. Ask for the 2025/26 CO2 saving under plan 4.6 with a range, and use it in the committee's decision (for example a
   reporting commitment the round must meet), so the season-level estimate carries weight in the answer.

These are general suggestions for strengthening the task, not a guarantee that it will be hard enough. Please test
your own model responses and only resubmit if at least two strong models still miss the answer.
